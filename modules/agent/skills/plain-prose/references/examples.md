# Before/after examples

Numbered to match the rules in SKILL.md. Most "before" lines are drawn from or modeled on Kristen Lowe's Work Bravely series and Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide.

## 1. Concrete nouns

Before: The reason he ran every day? Because he was building the physical infrastructure for a competitive tennis career.
After: He ran every day because professional tennis players need to be in good cardiovascular shape.

Before: This creates a more robust ecosystem for collaboration.
After: Techs can now see each other's tickets.

## 2. Intensifiers

Before: For the past six months, I have been quietly building the product that's going to change everything.
After: For the past six months I've been working with packaging designers and a formulator on a new product.

Before: This is a genuinely important distinction.
After: This distinction matters because the two cases get billed differently.

## 3. Plain verbs

Before: The way she articulated her response demonstrated her grasp of the third law of thermodynamics.
After: She understands it; she gave a clear explanation of the third law of thermodynamics.

Before: We leveraged existing tooling to facilitate the migration.
After: We used the existing scripts to run the migration.

## 4. Metaphor verbs

Before: The topic earns its place and really anchors the whole conversation.
After: It makes sense to put it on the agenda because it gives context for the other item.

Before: This surfaces a deeper issue with how we position the offering.
After: This shows the price page is confusing.

## 5. Uniform sentences

Before: The new restaurant opened its doors in March, roughly a month behind its original schedule. While early diners have offered mixed reviews of the menu, such challenges are fairly common for first-time restaurateurs. The owners have acknowledged the delay and expressed a commitment to refining the dining experience. It remains to be seen whether the restaurant can build a loyal following in the months ahead.
After: The new restaurant opened in March, a month late. The food isn't very good yet. That's understandable for owners who have never opened a restaurant before, and I'd give them a few months.

## 6. Negative parallelism

Before: Not only did the program reduce ticket volume, but it also improved response times.
After: The program reduced ticket volume and improved response times.

Before: It's not the tooling. It's not the staffing. It's the handoff between engineering and design.
After: The handoff between engineering and design is taking longer than we expected.

## 7. Tricolons

Before: The experience was fun, relaxing, and rejuvenating.
After: The trip was fun and relaxing.

Before: The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking opportunities.
After: The event has talks and panels, with time between them to meet people.

## 8. Question-and-answer fragments

Before: The beta results? Transformative. Adoption climbed 12% across participants.
After: Adoption rose 12% among users in the beta.

## 9. False ranges

Before: From teenagers to managing directors, ChatGPT has become an indispensable tool for most people.
After: Most people use ChatGPT in some form.

Before: Our journey has taken us from the singularity of the Big Bang to the grand cosmic web.
After: We've covered the Big Bang through the formation of galaxies.

## 10. Participle tails and nominalizations

Before: Candidates wait an average of nine days between their first and second interviews, underscoring the inefficiency in our process.
After: Candidates wait an average of nine days between their first and second interviews.

Before: The founding of the institute represented a significant shift toward regional statistical independence, enabling the region to develop a tailored system.
After: The institute was founded in 1989 so the region could publish its own statistics.

Before: The implementation of the backup power configuration was completed by the team.
After: The team installed the backup batteries.

## 11. Em dashes

Before: The current revision — well-sourced, policy-compliant, and collaboratively improved — no longer needs the banner.
After: The current revision is well sourced and no longer needs the banner.

## 13. One-line paragraphs

Before:
> Most companies think they have a productivity problem.
>
> They don't.
>
> They have a clarity problem.

After: Most companies that think they have a productivity problem actually have a clarity problem: nobody can say what the priority is.

## 14. Announcing and recapping

Before: Here's the thing: remote hires miss the passive learning that happens in an office. [...] This means that for remote teams, deliberate effort makes all the difference.
After: Remote hires miss the passive learning that happens in an office: how decisions get made, who to ask, when it's safe to push back. The companies that handle this well replace it on purpose, with onboarding buddies, written norms, and managers who over-explain.

## 15. Unnecessary bullets

Before:
> Several factors contribute to unproductive meetings:
> - Lack of agenda: Meetings without an agenda are often unfocused
> - Too many attendees: Large group meetings make it harder to reach decisions
> - No clear owner: When no one is responsible, follow-through suffers

After: Meetings go badly when they have no agenda, too many people, or nobody who owns the outcome.

## 16. Hedging

Before: On balance, both approaches have merit, and the right choice will depend on organizational context.
After: Use the shared queue. The only case where the per-tech queue wins is a site with one tech, and we don't have any.

## 17. Wider, not deeper

Before: Reading gives you access to people you'll never meet. Almost everything humans have figured out is stored in text. Without reading, you're limited to what's in the room with you.
After: Reading gives you access to people you'll never meet. Most of what has been worked out in the last 5,000 years was written down and never recorded any other way, so someone who can't read is limited to whoever is in the room.

## 18. Stakes

Before: Moving the sync to Wednesday will change everything about how the team operates.
After: Moving the sync to Wednesday gives people Monday and Tuesday to get work done before reporting on it.

## 21. Chatbot residue

Before: I hope this overview is helpful! Let me know if you'd like me to expand on any section.
After: [delete]

## 6 (antithesis without "not")

Before: The opt-out ideal was his; the opt-out life was not.
After: He preached the self-sufficient yeoman and never lived as one.

Before: Those aren't similar policies with different press. They're opposite policies on the one question that faction cares about.
After: On money the two policies were opposites, and money is the question that faction cares about.

## 13 and 13b (one-line opener, closing aphorism)

Before: The premise is half right, and the half that's wrong is the interesting half.
After: The premise is half right. The deficits were similar; the monetary policies were opposites, and that is where the villain story comes from.

Before (last sentence of a piece): On that reading the villain and the hero are the same kind of figure, and the sorting into good and bad is downstream of whose regime you'd rather live in.
After: [end the piece on the preceding factual sentence]

## 22 (unearned specificity)

Before: About 40 em dashes cut to zero.
After: Every em dash cut; I didn't count the originals.

Before: The tightest money since the 1920s.
After: The sharpest disinflation since the 1920s.

## Draft 2 in practice (closers extracted from one 400-word piece)

Listed on their own, the closers from a for/against piece on fossil fuels:

- "...a civilization that lost the energy would lose the machines with it."
- "Losing oil next Tuesday and losing it over sixty years are different questions with different answers."
- "...which is the question that matters and the one the theory skips."

In context each read as a reasonable ending. Side by side, all three are built the same way: a balanced pair delivered as a verdict. Fix: keep at most one, and end the other paragraphs on a fact ("...about half the people alive." / "...different answers at each timescale, and the theory doesn't say which it means.").

## Draft 1 counts (from a Star Trek economics piece)

Planned as "first paragraph longest": came out 9, 10, 9, 2. Three equal blocks and a recap.
Planned with numbers: 10, 4, 7, 0 (no recap paragraph; the piece ends on the last fact of paragraph three).

## Opener list (same piece)

- "The replicator doesn't abolish scarcity; it moves it."  (negative parallel)
- "The weak part is the Federation's own claim about itself."  (fine)
- "From outside, the Federation looks like what it is:"  (setup for a punch)
- "The economics hold up better than the politics."  (recap opener)

Fixes: "The replicator moves scarcity rather than removing it." / "Seen from a non-member world, the Federation is a bloc of about 150 planets that controls the dilithium, the trade lanes, and the fleet." / delete the last paragraph.
